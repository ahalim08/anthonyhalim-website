Who is the Customer, Really?

Examining how SaaS companies define and track customers. Why logos fail, and why MSA-based counting reveals the truth about churn, growth, and organizational complexity.

A few years ago, a census surveyor knocked on my door. My then 1-bedroom apartment in Los Angeles. I remember it being an unusually gray day for an LA summer. I stood in the doorway for maybe 15 minutes while they walked me through a short set of questions: how many people in the household, ages, backgrounds, any children. I added the dog as a half-joke. She didn't count.

My first thought when it was over: that was actually kind of cool.

Looking back, what strikes me is how much that 15-minute doorstep conversation turned into. It wasn't just "the Halims live here." It was two people in their 30s, one male, one female, no children. That specificity is what makes the census useful. It drives political representation, federal funding allocation, infrastructure planning, and a hundred other downstream decisions most people never think about. Counting households loses the granularity that makes the data useful. Counting the people inside them is what lets you actually run a country.

The same logic breaks down every day in SaaS. Only we call it "counting logos."

I know what people mean when they say "we just closed another logo." I still hate it.

A logo is a visual artifact — a .png file someone drops into a slide deck. A customer is a relationship with a budget owner, a renewal decision, and a set of obligations we owe them. When we collapse the latter into the former, we lose the same thing the census would lose if it only tracked addresses: the people inside, and what's actually true about them. That loss doesn't show up immediately. It shows up six months later in churn analysis that doesn't hold together, expansion stories that no one can explain cleanly, and metrics that feel right until someone asks one follow-up question.

Why "Logos" Break Down

I get why the shorthand persists. It's fast and it fits on a slide. The problem is it stops working the moment complexity shows up.

Go back to the census for a second. Every person in that household had their own agency over buying decisions. If I were a salesperson selling into the Halim household, knowing we were "the Halims" wasn't enough. I needed to understand Anthony's needs — and how they might have differed from Norma's. We probably agreed on some things and diverged completely on others. Same address. Same logo. Different customers.

Now scale that up by a few thousand employees and a few hundred million in revenue.

Large organizations rarely behave like a single customer. They have multiple buying centers operating on different budgets, different economic owners with different incentives, different problems they're trying to solve, and entirely different paths to renewal or expansion. Yet we treat all of that as one win because it's tidier that way. The result is that we blur together relationships that are fundamentally different from each other — and when one part of the organization grows and another stagnates, we genuinely can't tell why. When something churns, we argue about whether it counts.

That's not a reporting problem. It's a definition problem.

Here's what that looks like in practice. Say you sign an MSA with the finance team at a large enterprise (call them Apex Corp). Fourteen months later, the marketing org signs a separate MSA. Eight months after that, engineering comes on board. Under logo-based tracking, you have one customer: Apex Corp. Under MSA-based tracking, you have three — and they tell completely different stories. Finance just renewed without much friction. Marketing is barely using the product and the champion left three months ago. Engineering is two months in with high engagement and room to expand. If you're counting logos, your board slide says "Apex renewed." Neat. If you're counting MSAs, you know exactly what's healthy, what's at risk, and where to focus next quarter.

Defining the Customer at the MSA Level

The fix I keep coming back to is also the least glamorous one: define a customer as a distinct MSA-governed relationship.

An MSA is objective in a way that a "logo" never is — it either exists or it doesn't. It defines scope, responsibility, and commercial terms. More importantly, it represents a real economic relationship between two parties, not just a brand name we recognize.

The difference in what you can actually see is significant:

Logo-based MSA-based
Customer count 1 (Apex Corp) 3 (Finance, Marketing, Engineering)
ARR at risk Unknown — masked by the healthy relationships Isolated — $80K tied to the Marketing MSA
Expansion opportunity Unclear — "Apex feels flat" Visible — Engineering MSA is 2 months in with room to grow
Churn signal Hidden until it's too late Flagged early at the relationship level
Board narrative "Apex renewed" "Apex: 2 healthy, 1 at risk, 1 expanding"

Customer count becomes a set of contracts rather than a vibe. ARR rolls up cleanly. Churn has an actual denominator. It also forces go-to-market teams to think about relationships rather than corporate umbrellas. That means maintaining multiple CRM records under the same parent organization, which feels inefficient until you recognize that each record is a different buying motion, a different stakeholder, and a different definition of success. Treating them as one account doesn't simplify things — it just hides the complexity until it resurfaces somewhere inconvenient.

The Upside: Clarity and Expansion

This approach almost always increases your reported customer count, and that tends to make people uncomfortable. The discomfort is misplaced.

If one "logo" becomes three MSAs, you didn't inflate anything — you finally counted reality correctly. This is especially useful in land-and-expand models, where growth comes from entering new buying centers over time rather than upselling a single monolithic account. Each MSA becomes a clean landing point with its own expansion curve and health metrics. Instead of asking why a logo is flat, you can see exactly which relationship is stalling and which one is accelerating. That's the difference between a story and an actual insight.

The Tradeoff: More Operational Friction

I won't pretend this is free. Multiple MSAs mean more legal coordination, more CRM records, more discipline across sales ops and finance. That's the primary reason most teams don't bother.

Think about what it took to run a census. Tens of thousands of workers (maybe more) fanning out across the country, knocking on doors, talking to real people, collecting and recording individual datapoints at scale. By no means a small lift. But the U.S. government decided that granularity was worth the operational cost, because the decisions sitting on top of that data were too consequential to get wrong. The 2020 census cost $13.7 billion — about $42 per person counted. It guides $2.8 trillion in annual federal spending allocations.1 Not a bad return on a doorstep conversation.

The same logic applies here.

First, churn looks worse. Under logo-based counting, losing one MSA while keeping two others registers as a partial churn — the logo survives, so the story stays intact. Under MSA-based counting, that same event is a full customer churn. You still have the other two relationships, but the denominator doesn't care. If your board is used to seeing logo retention numbers, the first time you present MSA-based churn is going to require some explaining.

Second, MSA consolidation creates noise. It happens: two business units get reorganized, procurement forces everything under one contract, or the company just decides to simplify. When two MSAs merge into one, that shows up as a customer churn partially offset by expansion ARR, and your customer count goes down. Nothing actually deteriorated, but the numbers will imply that it did. The business is fine. The spreadsheet is confused. You'll need an asterisk in board discussions and a clear methodology for how you handle these events consistently.

Neither of these is a reason to abandon MSA-based counting, but they are reasons to get ahead of the narrative before someone else interprets the numbers for you. You can pay that cost upfront in process, tooling, and proactive framing, or you can pay it later in confused metrics, awkward board conversations, and decisions made on a partial picture of reality. At scale, clarity is almost always cheaper than ambiguity.

The Bottom Line

Every downstream metric — ARR, NRR, churn, CAC payback — rests on how you define the customer. If the definition is fuzzy, the conclusions built on top of it will be too.

An MSA-based definition isn't the kind of thing anyone puts in a press release. It's disciplined, a little boring, and operationally inconvenient. But it forces honesty about how customers actually behave — and in my experience, that kind of honesty compounds over time in ways that a convenient simplification never does.

You can't tell me she doesn't count...
