## North Star: Why These Articles Exist

In the age of AI, anyone can publish a topical or technical article. A model can explain in seconds why tracking signed vs. active bookings matters. What it cannot do is describe what it felt like to get that wrong — the organizational friction, the repeated conversations, the slow realization that a definition problem had become a trust problem.

Anthony's articles are built from **lived experience**. Not frameworks borrowed from textbooks. Not opinions formed from reading other people's opinions. Years of real decisions, real mistakes, and the hard-won clarity that comes after. That's the moat.

Every article should reflect this. The goal isn't to cover a topic — it's to offer a lesson the reader could only get from someone who was actually there. When the writing could have been generated by a model in three seconds, something has gone wrong. When it could only have been written by someone who sat in those rooms, had those conversations, and felt that particular kind of professional embarrassment — that's the target.

**The test:** Could an AI have written this from training data alone? If yes, it needs more of Anthony in it.

## Article Structure: The Persuasion Arc

Every article should follow this arc, whether or not the headers make it explicit:

### 1. The Distinction
What most people assume or do, and why it's imprecise or wrong. Lead here — create friction that makes the reader want an answer.

### 2. The Real Problem
What actually breaks when you don't make the distinction. Be specific: the organizational friction, the repeated conversations, the moment you realized it was a trust problem, not a process problem.

### 3. The Fix or Reframe
The cleaner mental model, definition, or approach. This is usually simpler than people expect.

### 4. The Tradeoffs
Honest acknowledgment of complexity. **Non-negotiable.** No article should pretend the fix is free. Name specific costs: operational, metric-level, political.

### 5. The Bottom Line
Why any of this matters. Stakes, not summary. What does the reader's organization look like if they get this right? Wrong?

## Tone of Voice: Register 3

### Lead with a Claim, Not Context

**❌ Weak:**
- "One of the most common mistakes in FP&A is..."
- "It's widely acknowledged that companies often struggle with..."

**✅ Strong:**
- "The most common mistake in FP&A is..."
- "Companies get this wrong."

### Sentence Rhythm: Intentional Variation

Pattern: **short punch → elaboration → short punch**

Not staccato throughout. Not flowing throughout. The variation is intentional.

**Example:**
"The model breaks. You're tracking signed bookings, but the sales team is forecasting active ones — different time windows, different definitions, same report. Six weeks later, you're explaining variance to the board and realizing the discrepancy was baked in from the start."

### First-Person Authority

Use first-person voice to signal direct experience, not hedging.

**✅ Authority:**
- "I keep coming back to..."
- "In my experience..."
- "I think the question worth asking is..."

**❌ Hedging:**
- "It could be argued that..."
- "One might say..."
- "Some people believe..."

### No Consulting-Speak, No Academic Hedging

**❌ Consulting-speak:**
- "At a high level, the stakeholders need to align on..."
- "There are a few key levers to consider..."
- "One could observe that..."

**✅ Direct:**
- "Leadership needs to agree on the number."
- "Three things matter."
- "Here's what happened."

### Headers Carry Arguments, Not Just Labels

**✅ Argumentative headers:**
- "That's not a reporting problem. It's a definition problem."
- "Why Simpler Is Actually Harder"
- "The Cost of Ambiguity"

**❌ Label headers:**
- "Background"
- "The Problem"
- "Solutions"
- "Overview"

### Name Failures Directly

Don't hide behind vagueness. Specific failure details build credibility more than general acknowledgment.

**❌ Vague:**
- "I've seen teams make this mistake."

**✅ Specific:**
- "I was the FP&A Director getting eviscerated on an audit committee call. All I could think about was how dumb I must have looked."

## Vulnerability and Narrative Texture

### Earned Self-Deprecation

Surface specific moments of failure, not general acknowledgment that mistakes happen. The detail is the proof.

**❌ False modesty:**
- "I've seen teams get this wrong"

**✅ Genuine credibility:**
- "I was the FP&A Director getting eviscerated on an audit committee call, and all I could think about was how dumb I must have looked."

The embarrassing specifics — the room, the feeling, the moment of realization — are what make the analytical point land. Vague humility reads as false modesty. Specific humility reads as truth.

### Hooks That Lower Resistance

For narrative-driven articles, the opener doesn't have to start with the finance topic. A scene, a pop culture reference, or a personal moment can draw the reader in before you pivot to the argument.

**Rule:** The hook has to earn its place by making the pivot feel surprising and inevitable. If it's just warmup, cut it.

(This applies to narrative articles only — analytical or methodological pieces should still lead with the claim.)

### Charitable Counterpoints

Name the take you're not supposed to have, then defend it. This is a credibility move.

**Example:**
"Everyone in the room was dunking on this person. But I think they were right. Here's why..."

## Humor and Voice Devices

Four techniques worth deploying deliberately. Each requires the right setup — used arbitrarily, they land flat.

### 1. Scale-Anchor Absurd Numbers

When a ratio or figure is the point, translate it once to a human-scale comparison that makes the absurdity legible. The comparison does more work than the number alone.

**Example:**
"A 2:1 debt-to-equity ratio is like carrying a mortgage equal to the total value of your house, then borrowing that amount again to buy a second one next door."

Use once per article, only when the number would otherwise scroll past.

### 2. Single-Word Reactions After Disclosures

A one-word or short-phrase response right after a data point or quote skips the commentary paragraph and trusts the reader.

**Example:**
"They'd been running the same forecast model for four years. Womp womp."

Works because it signals the author has already processed the detail. One per article maximum — only when the reaction IS the analysis, not a setup for it.

### 3. Tonal Whiplash as a Credibility Device

The heaviest analytical sentence earns the lightest punchline. A writer comfortable enough with complex material to be briefly irreverent about it reads as expert, not sloppy.

**Rule:** The device only works if the heavy setup is there. A punchline on thin analysis reads as mugging for laughs.

Use once per article, positioned after the most complex or damning data point.

### 4. Extended Analogies for Mechanisms

When an article explains *why* something works (not just what it is), invest in one extended analogy — 2–4 sentences that make the mechanism tactile.

A strong analogy eliminates the need for five supporting data points. Relevant in FP&A articles when explaining why a metric design produces certain organizational behaviors.

## Length

**Target: 800–1,200 words** of body copy (excluding title, category label, and date)

This is the right range for a casual professional reader who wants a "quick download":
- Long enough to make a real argument with supporting logic
- Short enough to read in under 6 minutes

Below 800 risks feeling thin. Above 1,500 starts losing the target reader.

## Concrete Examples and Tables

Every article needs at least one of the following to make the argument tangible:

### Concrete Example

A specific, named hypothetical (e.g., "Apex Corp") that walks through the scenario in enough detail that the reader can picture it.

Not: "imagine a company that..."  
Yes: Give it a name, give it specifics, make it feel real.

One paragraph is enough.

### Comparison Table

Use when the article is arguing that two approaches produce different outcomes.

**Rules:**
- Tables should be load-bearing — show the reader something they couldn't easily absorb in prose
- Columns should be "what you see" or "what you can do" under each method, not just labels
- Reference example: the "Who Is the Customer" table

Use whichever is more appropriate for the content type:
- **Definitional or methodological articles:** tables usually work
- **Narrative or experience-driven articles:** concrete examples usually work better

When in doubt, use both.

## Bolded Accent Lines

Every article should have **3–4 lines bolded in the site accent color** (`#c84a1f`):

```html
<strong style="color: #c84a1f">Line goes here.</strong>
```

These lines are chosen so that a skimmer reading only the bolded text gets the complete argument. Select them to follow the persuasion arc:

| Arc Position | What the Line Should Do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The distinction | State the core claim or reframe in one sentence | "That's not a reporting problem. It's a definition problem." |
| The real problem | Name the failure or cost of the current approach | "Every definition ambiguity becomes an organizational trust problem." |
| The fix | State why the solution isn't what people assume | "The answer is simpler than you think." |
| The bottom line | Land the stakes — why this matters | "How a company talks about its numbers determines everything else." |

**Rules:**
- No more than 4. Fewer is fine if the article is short
- The bolded lines should be able to stand alone — not mid-sentence fragments
- Not setup lines that require context
- Vary placement throughout the article; avoid clustering all 4 in one section

## Additional Considerations

### Opening Hook

The first paragraph should create enough friction that the reader wants to keep going. The best openers state something that most people would agree with on the surface but that the article is about to complicate.

Scene-setting openers are appropriate for **narrative and experience-driven articles only** — and only if the scene itself is the point. If removing the scene loses meaning, it earns its place. If it's just warmup, cut it.

### Section Endings

The last sentence of each section should either land a point cleanly or raise a question that the next section answers. Sections should not just trail off.

A short, declarative closer is almost always better than a transitional sentence.

**Example:**
"That's not a reporting problem. It's a definition problem."

### Tradeoffs Are Non-Negotiable

Every article that advocates for a method or approach must include an honest tradeoffs section. Omitting it reads as naive or promotional.

The tradeoffs section should name **specific costs** — operational, metric-level, political — not just say "this requires more work."

### Category Tagging

Each article maps to one primary category on the site:
- SaaS Metrics
- FP&A
- Budgeting
- Scaling Finance
- Los Angeles / Civic Finance

Keep the argument scoped to that category — don't let articles drift into adjacent topics without a clear reason.

### File Naming

Follow the global convention: `YYYY-MM-DD-descriptive-name.md`

Example: `2026-05-13-content-ideas.md`

### File Location

All content files must be saved to the `Content/` folder:
```
Content/
├── 2026-02-03-who-is-the-customer.md
├── 2026-05-02-fpa-scaling-part-1.md
└── Los Angeles/
    └── (LA series articles)
```

Do not save articles to the root project folder or any other location.

## Series Articles

If writing a multi-part series:

### Include a Tease in the Final Paragraph
One sentence naming the next part and why it's different.

**Example:**
"Part II explores how these principles scale when you hit $50M ARR — and where they break."

### Link Previous Parts
Include a brief note at the top linking to the previous article.

### Update the Backlog
Update the series outline in the content-ideas file with completion status.
