# Tone & Voice Guidelines — Register 3

**Source:** Personal website content standards for Anthony Halim  
**Last updated:** May 13, 2026  
**Scope:** All published articles and professional content

---

## Overview

Register 3 is the tonal register for **professional content written from direct experience**. It's authoritative without being distant, conversational without being casual, and specific without being overly technical.

The voice signals: "I've been in those rooms. I know what I'm talking about. I'm willing to look foolish to make the point."

---

## Core Principles

### 1. Lead with Claims, Not Context

**Don't warm the reader up.** State something that forces them to have an opinion.

**❌ Weak openers (too much context):**
- "It's widely acknowledged that..."
- "One of the most important aspects of FP&A is..."
- "In today's fast-moving environment, many companies struggle with..."
- "There are a number of reasons why..."

**✅ Strong openers (direct claims):**
- "Most companies get this wrong."
- "The mistake is almost universal."
- "Here's what breaks."

**Why it works:** A reader facing a claim either agrees or disagrees — either way, they're engaged. Context puts them in passive mode.

---

### 2. Active Voice and Concrete Language

Replace passive constructions and vague abstractions with active, specific language.

**❌ Passive/vague:**
- "There was no clear definition"
- "It can be seen that mistakes often occur"
- "There are several reasons why this happens"
- "Weaknesses in the system were observed"

**✅ Active/concrete:**
- "Nobody defined it."
- "People get it wrong."
- "Three things cause this."
- "We saw the system break."

**Exception:** Passive voice is sometimes better when:
- The actor is genuinely unimportant ("The system was built in 2019")
- You want to soften accountability gracefully (rare; prefer direct language)

---

### 3. First-Person Authority

Use first-person voice to signal direct experience. Don't hide behind passive observation or hedging.

**✅ Authority through first-person:**
- "I keep coming back to..."
- "In my experience..."
- "I think the question worth asking is..."
- "I was wrong about this."
- "I watched this play out three times."

**❌ Hedging distance:**
- "It could be argued that..."
- "One might say..."
- "Some people believe..."
- "It's possible that..."

**Why it works:** First-person grounds the argument in lived reality. Hedging language signals uncertainty and creates distance.

---

### 4. Sentence Rhythm: Short Punch → Elaboration → Short Punch

Vary sentence length and structure deliberately. The pattern creates momentum and emphasis.

**Example (strong rhythm):**
"The model breaks. You're tracking signed bookings, but the sales team is forecasting active ones — different time windows, different definitions, same report. Six weeks later, you're explaining variance to the board and realizing the discrepancy was baked in from the start."

**Structure:**
- Short punch: "The model breaks."
- Elaboration: "You're tracking signed bookings... same report."
- Short punch: "Six weeks later, you're explaining variance..."

**What this rhythm does:**
- Opens with impact
- Explains with detail
- Closes with resonance

**Anti-pattern (weak rhythm):**
- All short sentences: staccato, exhausting
- All long sentences: flowing, but no emphasis
- Uniform paragraph length: templated, forgettable

---

### 5. Headers Carry Arguments, Not Just Labels

Section headers should make a claim or pose a question that advances the argument. They should never be pure labels.

**✅ Argumentative headers:**
- "That's not a reporting problem. It's a definition problem."
- "Why Simpler Is Actually Harder"
- "The Cost of Ambiguity"
- "Where Most Teams Get Stuck"
- "Why the Fix Isn't What You Think It Is"

**❌ Label headers (avoid):**
- "Background"
- "The Problem"
- "Solutions"
- "Overview"
- "Why This Matters"
- "Conclusion"

**Why it matters:** Argumentative headers guide the reader through the logic. A skimmer reading only headers should understand the core claim.

---

### 6. Name Failures Directly

Don't hide behind vagueness. Specific failure details build credibility more than general acknowledgment. Details are the proof.

**❌ Vague:**
- "I've seen teams make this mistake."
- "Companies often struggle with this."
- "It's easy to get wrong."

**✅ Specific:**
- "I was the FP&A Director getting eviscerated on an audit committee call, and all I could think about was how dumb I must have looked."
- "The spreadsheet was updated on Tuesday, but the VP of Sales was presenting yesterday's version."
- "We showed the board a $2M variance and couldn't explain why it existed."

The embarrassing specifics — the room, the people, the feeling, the moment of realization — are what make analytical points land. Vague humility reads as false modesty. Specific humility reads as truth.

## Pitfalls to Hunt During the Tone-Voice Pass

These patterns recur and dilute the voice. Check for all of them:

### 1. Passive Constructions

**❌ Passive:**
- "There was no X"
- "There were Y people"
- "It is believed that..."
- "The mistake was made"

**✅ Active:**
- "No X."
- "Y people"
- "I think..."
- "We got it wrong."

---

### 2. Hedged Claim Openers

**❌ Hedged:**
- "One of the most important things..."
- "It could be argued that..."
- "Some might say..."
- "It's possible that..."

**✅ Direct:**
- "The most important thing..."
- "Here's why..."
- "It matters because..."
- "This happens because..."

---

### 3. Label-Colon Overuse

Limit label-colon constructions to one per section. Two in the same section reads as templated.

**❌ Over-templated:**
"The fix has three components: First, clarity. Second, alignment. Third, discipline."

**✅ Better:**
"The fix is simpler than you'd think: one definition, documented clearly, shared across the org."

---

### 4. Redundant Audience Address

Don't signal who the piece is for twice in the intro.

**❌ Redundant:**
"If you're a finance leader... For anyone in finance... As a CFO, you know that..."

**✅ Single signal (implied throughout):**
Write as if speaking to peers who don't need basics explained. Assumption is built into vocabulary and examples.

---

### 5. Setup Phrases That Delay Substance

**❌ Delaying:**
- "But here's what X buys you:"
- "The key insight is:"
- "What this means is:"
- "So what's the solution?"

**✅ Lead with substance directly:**
Instead of "What this means is: you need better data," just write: "You need better data."

---

### 6. Uniform Paragraph Length

Vary short and long deliberately. Short = punchy. Long = deep explanation.

**❌ All medium (monotonous):**
Every paragraph is 3–4 sentences. No variation. Feels processed.

**✅ Varied:**
Mix 1–2 sentence paragraphs with 5–6 sentence paragraphs. Rhythm does the work.

---

### 7. "Plucky Cadence" Overuse

Short, staccato parallel fragments used as mic-drops.

**Examples:**
- "Same address. Same logo. Different customers."
- "That's not a reporting problem. It's a definition problem."
- "Build it twice. Get it right the third time."

**Rule:** Limit to **2 instances per article maximum**

More than 2 reads as a stylistic tic and loses impact. When in doubt, fold the fragments into a single sentence.

**Example of folding:**
- ❌ "Same address. Same logo. Different customers." (if already used plucky cadence elsewhere)
- ✅ "Same address and logo, but different customers."

---

### 8. Em-Dash Overuse

Limit em-dashes to instances where no other punctuation works as well.

**Preference hierarchy:**
1. **Colon** — introduce a list or explanation
2. **Period** — split a pivot into two sentences
3. **Parentheses** — asides
4. **Comma** — conjunctions and continuations
5. **Em-dash** — extreme asides or dramatic pauses (rare)

**Note:** Accent lines are exempt from this rule.

**Process:** During the tone-voice pass, flag every em-dash and ask whether a cleaner substitute exists before keeping it.

**❌ Over-reliant on em-dashes:**
"The key to FP&A — and what most people get wrong — is that you need clean definitions — otherwise, everything breaks."

**✅ Better:**
"The key to FP&A is clean definitions. Most people get this wrong. Without them, everything breaks."

---

### 9. Unnecessary Hedging Words

Limit to instances where genuine uncertainty exists.

**❌ Over-hedged:**
- "might"
- "could"
- "perhaps"
- "somewhat"
- "arguably"
- "in some sense"
- "it's possible that"
- "one could say"

**✅ Use only when true uncertainty exists:**
- "I don't know if this is universal, but..."
- "Some teams handle this differently..."
- "I could be wrong, but..."

---

### 10. Vague Attribution

Be specific about what you observed and when.

**❌ Vague:**
- "I've seen teams get this wrong"
- "In my experience..."
- "Most companies struggle with..."

**✅ Specific:**
- "At AuditBoard, we were tracking signed bookings when the board wanted active ones. Took six months to untangle."
- "I watched three startups grow from $20M to $100M, and all three hit the same wall here."
- "The audit committee call where I had to explain the variance — that's when I realized we'd defined the problem wrong."

Specificity is credibility.

## Voice in Practice: Worked Example

**Topic:** Why signed vs. active bookings matter

**❌ Weak version (passive, hedged, vague):**
"There are a number of reasons why it's important to distinguish between signed and active bookings. In many cases, teams struggle with this distinction. There are often definitional ambiguities that can lead to confusion. It's possible that this could result in forecast variance."

**✅ Strong version (active, specific, authoritative):**
"Signed and active bookings are not the same thing, and that distinction costs most teams two to three months of painful variance meetings every quarter. Here's how it breaks: the sales team closes a deal on the first day of the month (signed). The customer doesn't start using the product until day fifteen (active). Your forecast assumes start-of-month, theirs assumes mid-month. You're both right, but you're looking at different numbers. And nobody realizes it until the close."

**What changed:**
- ❌ "There are reasons..." → ✅ "This costs teams..."
- ❌ "Teams struggle..." → ✅ "Here's how it breaks..."
- ❌ Passive/vague → ✅ Active, specific, vivid
- ❌ No first-person authority → ✅ Implied authority through specificity
- ❌ Weak headers → ✅ Strong opening claim

## Testing Your Voice

### Test 1: Can This Be Read as a Claim?

Read your opening paragraph. Does it state something the reader either agrees or disagrees with? If it just provides context, rewrite it to lead with the claim.

### Test 2: Could an AI Have Written This?

If your article could be generated from training data alone, it needs more lived experience woven in. Specific moments, failures, rooms, reactions — those can't be simulated.

### Test 3: Does Every Header Carry an Argument?

Read only the headers. Do they form a logical argument? If headers are just labels, rewrite them as claims or questions.

### Test 4: Are Sentences Varied in Length?

Count sentence lengths in a paragraph. Vary them. If all sentences are 15+ words, break some up. If all are 5 words or fewer, combine some.

### Test 5: Is Passive Voice Justified?

Find every passive construction. Ask: Is the actor truly unimportant? If not, rewrite in active voice.

---

## Checklist: Tone-Voice Pass

- [ ] No passive constructions (except where grammatically required)
- [ ] All hedged claim openers replaced with direct statements
- [ ] Label-colons limited to one per section
- [ ] Redundant audience address removed
- [ ] Setup phrases replaced with direct substance
- [ ] Paragraph length varied (short ↔ long)
- [ ] Plucky cadence limited to 2 instances per article
- [ ] Every em-dash reviewed and justified
- [ ] Headers carry arguments, not labels
- [ ] First-person voice is active and authoritative
- [ ] Specific failures named (not vague generalizations)
- [ ] Unnecessary hedging removed
